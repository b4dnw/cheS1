### ⚡ Unit 1: Electrochemistry & Sensors

#### 🔋 Cells

| Property | Galvanic Cell | Electrolytic Cell |
|---|---|---|
| Energy Conversion | Chemical -> Electrical | Electrical -> Chemical |

| ΔG | -ΔG | +ΔG |
| Anode | - | + |
| Cathode | + | - |

#### 🎯 Reference Electrodes

| Electrode | Type | Details |
|---|---|---|
| **SHE** | Primary | Standard Hydrogen Electrode. Exactly 0.00 V. Uses H2 gas at 1 atm and 1M H+. |
| **Ag/AgCl** | Secondary | Silver wire coated in AgCl, dipped in KCl. Cheap, non-toxic, highly stable. |
| **SCE** | Secondary | Saturated Calomel Electrode. Uses Mercury/Mercurous chloride. Produces exactly 0.2422 V at 298 K. |

#### 📡 Sensors

| Sensor Type | Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Potentiometric** | Measures Voltage (EMF) at zero current. Relies on the Nernst equation. | Glass Electrode for pH |
| **Amperometric** | Applies constant voltage, measures resulting Faradaic current. | Clark Oxygen Sensor |

#### 🔌 Batteries

| Battery | Class | Details |
|---|---|---|
| **Alkali Metal Sulphide (Li-S)** | Primary | High capacity, light, but suffers from polysulfide shuttle effect. |
| **Ni-MH** | Secondary | Metal hydride anode, KOH electrolyte. High density, no toxic cadmium. |

#### ⛽ Fuel Cells

Continuous power, zero emissions. Propane & Methanol cells both deliver 1.20 V.

### 💎 Unit 2: Solid State & Crystallography

#### 🧊 4 Types of Crystalline Solids

| # | Type | Example | Properties |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ionic | NaCl | Electrostatic forces, hard, conducts when molten |
| 2 | Metallic | Copper | Sea of electrons, great conductors |
| 3 | Covalent | Diamond | Continuous covalent bonds, hard insulators |
| 4 | Molecular | Ice | Van der Waals forces, soft, low melting points |

#### 🏗️ Crystal Architecture

Defined by 6 Lattice Parameters (edges a, b, c and angles α, β, γ). There are exactly 7 Crystal Systems and 14 Bravais Lattices.

#### ✨ X-Rays & Z-1 Rule

Electrons hit a target (like Cu, Z=29) to make X-rays. To get a pure beam, use a filter with one less proton (Z-1). A Nickel filter (Z=28) absorbs the messy K_beta rays from Cu.

#### 🌈 Liquid Crystals

Flows like liquid, ordered like a solid (Anisotropic).

| Phase | Structure |
|---|---|
| Nematic | Parallel, no layers |
| Smectic | Parallel, neat layers |
| Cholesteric | Helical layers, change color with temp |

## 🧮 Section 2: Important Formulas & Definitions

### 📐 Formulas

| Name | Formula |
|---|---|
| Standard Cell Potential | `E°_cell = E°_cathode - E°_anode` |
| Nernst Equation (298 K) | `E_cell = E°_cell - (0.0591 / n) * log([Anode] / [Cathode])` |
| Gibbs Free Energy | `ΔG = -n * F * E_cell (F = 96500 C)` |
| Bragg's Law | `nλ = 2d * sin(θ)` |
| Born-Haber Equation | `ΔH_f = ΔH_sub + ΔH_IE + ½ΔH_diss + ΔH_EA + U` |

### 📖 Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Electrode** | A conductive material allowing electron flow into/out of an electrochemical cell. |
| **Fuel Cell** | A device converting the chemical energy of a continuously supplied fuel and oxidant directly into electricity. |
| **Lattice Energy (U)** | The energy released when gaseous ions form one mole of a solid ionic compound at 298 K. |
| **Cohesive Energy** | The energy required to completely atomize a solid crystal into isolated free atoms at absolute zero (0 K). |
| **Anisotropy** | Physical/optical properties changing depending on the direction of measurement. |

<summary><strong>⚡ Electrodes & Reference Electrodes (6 Questions)</strong></summary>

<br>

**Q: What is an electrode? Why do we use them?**

A: An electrode is a conductive material (like platinum or carbon) that allows the flow of electrons into or out of an electrochemical system. We use electrodes to bridge the gap between the chemical solution (electrolyte) and the external electrical circuit, providing a physical surface for oxidation and reduction reactions to occur.

---

**Q: What is a reference electrode? Write its characteristics and advantages.**

A: A reference electrode maintains a stable, reproducible, and known potential, used as a standard to measure the potential of other working electrodes. Characteristics: Constant potential, non-polarizable, chemically inert. Advantages: Provides a highly reliable baseline for accurate measurements without interfering with the reaction.

---

**Q: Explain the Saturated Calomel Electrode (SCE) and its working principle.**

A: The SCE is a widely used secondary reference electrode. It consists of elemental mercury mixed with mercurous chloride (calomel), which is then immersed in a saturated potassium chloride (KCl) solution. It provides a highly stable, reproducible standard potential of +0.2422 V at 298 K. Its potential depends entirely on the concentration of the KCl solution.

---

**Q: Why is the Ag/AgCl electrode commonly used? Explain its construction and working.**

A: It is inexpensive, completely non-toxic, highly stable, and requires little maintenance. Construction: A solid silver (Ag) wire coated with silver chloride (AgCl), immersed in a saturated potassium chloride (KCl) solution. Working Principle: It operates on a reversible redox reaction based on chloride ion concentration: AgCl(s) + e⁻ ↔ Ag(s) + Cl⁻(aq).

---

**Q: Explain the working of a glass electrode for pH measurement. What are its limitations?**

A: A glass electrode consists of a thin, hydrogen-ion-sensitive glass membrane filled with an internal buffer (HCl) and an internal Ag/AgCl reference electrode. When immersed in a solution, a potential develops across the glass membrane that depends purely on the H⁺ concentration (pH). Limitations: The glass is highly fragile, has high electrical resistance, and exhibits "alkaline error" in highly basic solutions.

---

**Q: Compare the Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE) with the Ag/AgCl electrode.**

A: SHE is the primary reference standard (exactly 0.00 V). Requires bubbling highly flammable H₂ gas at 1 atm into a 1M H⁺ solution. It is difficult to construct, hazardous, and fragile. Ag/AgCl is a secondary reference (~0.197 V). It is a compact, liquid-filled tube that is safe, non-toxic, and much easier to handle in daily laboratory use.

<summary><strong>📡 Sensors (3 Questions)</strong></summary>

<br>

**Q: What are potentiometric sensors? Explain with the glass electrode example.**

A: Potentiometric sensors measure the voltage (EMF) between a working and reference electrode while drawing virtually zero current. The voltage changes logarithmically with the target ion's concentration (via the Nernst equation). Example: The Glass Electrode is a potentiometric sensor that specifically measures H⁺ ions to determine pH.

---

**Q: Explain amperometric sensors with the example of a Clark oxygen electrode.**

A: Amperometric sensors apply a constant voltage and measure the resulting Faradaic current generated by the redox reaction of the analyte. The measured current is directly proportional to the analyte's concentration. Example: The Clark oxygen electrode reduces dissolved O₂ at a platinum cathode, generating a current to measure oxygen levels in blood or water. Key Features: High sensitivity, rapid response, measures current.

---

**Q: Differentiate between potentiometric and amperometric sensors.**

A: 1. Measurement: Potentiometric measures Voltage (EMF); Amperometric measures Current. 2. Current Flow: Potentiometric draws zero current; Amperometric relies on continuous current flow. 3. Example: Potentiometric = Glass pH electrode; Amperometric = Clark Oxygen Sensor or Glucose biosensor.

</details>

<summary><strong>🔋 Batteries & Fuel Cells (4 Questions)</strong></summary>

<br>

**Q: What are alkali metal sulphide batteries? Explain construction, working, and limitations.**

A: High-energy cells using an alkali metal as the anode and a sulphide-compound as the cathode. Reactions (Li-S): Anode: Li → Li⁺ + e⁻. Cathode: S + 2Li⁺ + 2e⁻ → Li₂S. Advantages: Exceptionally high theoretical capacity, low cost of sulfur, lightweight. Limitations: The "polysulfide shuttle effect," where intermediate sulfur compounds dissolve into the electrolyte and ruin the battery lifespan.

---

**Q: Explain the construction, working, and reactions of a Ni-MH battery. Compare it with Ni-Cd.**

A: Rechargeable secondary cell. Construction: Anode is a Hydrogen-absorbing Metal Hydride (MH). Cathode is Nickel Oxyhydroxide (NiOOH). Electrolyte is alkaline KOH. Reactions: MH + NiO(OH) ↔ M + Ni(OH)₂ (Left-to-right is discharge, right-to-left is charge). Comparison to Ni-Cd: Ni-MH has higher energy capacity, suffers less "memory effect," and is eco-friendly because it contains zero toxic Cadmium.

---

**Q: Define a fuel cell. Explain the Hydrogen-Oxygen fuel cell.**

A: A device that continuously converts the chemical energy of a supplied fuel and oxidant directly into electricity. Hydrogen-Oxygen Cell: H₂ gas is pumped to the anode, O₂ to the cathode, separated by KOH electrolyte. Anode: 2H₂ + 4OH⁻ → 4H₂O + 4e⁻. Cathode: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻. Net: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.

---

**Q: Write the overall reactions for Methanol and Propane fuel cells. List advantages.**

A: Propane: C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O. Methanol: CH₃OH + 3/2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Both produce 1.20 V. Advantages: High efficiency, zero harmful emissions (eco-friendly), no recharging downtime. Used in space vehicles and submarines.

</details>

<summary><strong>💎 Solid State & Crystallography (7 Questions)</strong></summary>

<br>

**Q: Define solids. Differentiate between crystalline and amorphous solids.**

A: Solids have fixed shape and volume, high density, and incompressibility. Crystalline: Well-defined long-range order, sharp melting points, anisotropic, form clean cuts upon cleavage (e.g., NaCl). Amorphous: Lacks long-range order, softens gradually over a temperature range, isotropic, breaks with irregular surfaces (e.g., Glass).

---

**Q: Explain the classification of crystalline solids based on the nature of intermolecular forces with examples.**

A: Crystalline solids are classified into four categories: 1) Ionic Solids: Held by strong electrostatic forces; hard, brittle, conduct electricity only when molten (e.g., NaCl). 2) Metallic Solids: Metal cations in a "sea of delocalized electrons"; excellent thermal/electrical conductors (e.g., Copper). 3) Covalent Network Solids: Held by continuous covalent bonds forming a giant network; extremely hard insulators (e.g., Diamond, Quartz). 4) Molecular Solids: Held by weak Van der Waals or dipole forces; soft with low melting points (e.g., Ice).

---

**Q: How are X-rays generated? Explain the Z-1 rule and filters.**

A: X-rays are generated by heating a tungsten filament to emit high-speed electrons, which are accelerated and smashed into a heavy metal target. To obtain a pure monochromatic beam for diffraction, a filter made of an element with one less atomic number (Z-1) is used. Example: A Nickel filter (Z=28) is used to perfectly absorb the unwanted high-energy K_beta rays from a Copper target (Z=29), leaving a pure K_alpha beam.

---

**Q: Derive Bragg's equation.**

A: When X-rays hit a crystal, they reflect off atomic planes. For constructive interference to occur, the extra distance traveled by the deeper wave must equal a whole number of wavelengths. Equation: nλ = 2d * sin(θ).

---

**Q: Define lattice parameters and write the seven crystal systems.**

A: Lattice parameters describe the size and shape of a 3D unit cell, consisting of three edge lengths (a, b, c) and three axial angles (α, β, γ). Seven Systems: Cubic, Tetragonal, Orthorhombic, Monoclinic, Triclinic, Hexagonal, Rhombohedral.

---

**Q: What is the Born-Haber cycle?**

A: A thermochemical cycle based on Hess's Law used to calculate the Lattice Energy of an ionic solid. It breaks down the formation of a solid into measurable hypothetical steps: Sublimation, Ionization, Dissociation, Electron Affinity, and Lattice Formation.

---

**Q: Define liquid crystals and mention their applications.**

A: A state of matter that flows like a fluid but maintains the ordered molecular alignment of a solid (anisotropic). Applications: LCD screens, digital thermometers, tunable optical lenses, and smart windows.
